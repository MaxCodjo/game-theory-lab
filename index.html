Game theory is the physics of strategic interaction

Whenever your best move depends on someone else's move — a rival's price, a negotiator's patience, a goalkeeper's dive — you're inside a game. Game theory gives you the map: players, strategies, payoffs, and the equilibria where everything settles.

01

Nash Equilibrium

stable balance · no one wants to move alone

Use for: general strategic prediction

Two coffee shops open across the street from each other. Each wants a price that pulls in customers — but undercut too hard and the other retaliates. After a few rounds, both settle at a price where neither can do better by changing alone. That resting point is the Nash equilibrium.

A strategy profile \(s^*\) is a Nash equilibrium if no player gains by deviating unilaterally:

$$u_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}^*) \ge u_i(s_i, s_{-i}^*) \quad \forall i,\ \forall s_i$$

\(s_{-i}^*\) = everyone else's strategies, held fixed.

Reach for it whenever you need to predict where a strategic situation will settle: price wars, arms races, traffic routes, standards adoption. It's the default solution concept of modern economics.

02

Prisoner's Dilemma

temptation to defect · private incentives hurt the group

Use for: cooperation problems & trust

Two farmers share a canal. If both conserve water, both harvests thrive. But each is tempted to take extra — even though that drains the other's field. Temptation wins, both overuse the canal, and both end up worse than if they'd cooperated.

Payoffs ordered \(T > R > P > S\): defection dominates, yet \((C,C)\) beats \((D,D)\).

$$\begin{array}{c|cc} & C & D \\ \hline C & (R,R) & (S,T) \\ D & (T,S) & (P,P) \end{array}$$

Temptation \(T\), Reward \(R\), Punishment \(P\), Sucker \(S\).

Climate agreements, cartel cheating, doping in sport, overfishing, office free-riding. Any time individual incentives quietly sabotage the group outcome.

03

Repeated Games

the future disciplines the present

Use for: reputation & long-term deals

Two suppliers compete for the same retailer every month. One considers cutting quality to save money — but knows the retailer will remember. Because they'll do business for years, both behave better than in a one-shot deal. The shadow of the future changes today.

The stage game repeats at \(t=0,1,2,\dots\); payoffs are discounted by \(\delta\):

$$U_i = (1-\delta)\sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \delta^t\, g_i(a^t), \qquad 0 \le \delta < 1$$

Patient players (high \(\delta\)) can sustain cooperation that one-shot logic destroys.

Supplier relationships, alliances, cartels, tit-for-tat diplomacy, online seller ratings. Whenever the same players will meet again.

04

Zero-Sum Games

one wins exactly what the other loses

Use for: pure competition

Two chess players sit down. Every advantage one gains makes the other exactly worse off. No shared profit, no win-win — only victory and defeat. The purest form of competition.

Payoffs always sum to zero:

$$u_1(s_1,s_2) + u_2(s_1,s_2) = 0 \;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\; u_2 = -u_1$$

Solved by minimax: maximize your worst case, because their gain is your loss.

Sports, chess, poker heads-up, sealed-bid contests, adversarial security. If a deal could grow the pie, it's not zero-sum — a common real-world mistake.

05

Sequential Games

move order is strategy

Use for: negotiations, entry, auctions

A challenger wants to enter a new market — but first watches whether the incumbent slashes prices to scare it off. The incumbent moves, the challenger reacts, and the outcome hangs on that order. Strategy isn't just what to do; it's when.

Modeled in extensive form as a game tree:

$$G = \big(N,\, H,\, P,\, \{A(h)\}_{h\in H},\, \{u_i\}_{i\in N}\big)$$

Histories \(H\), player function \(P\), actions \(A(h)\). Solve by backward induction: start at the leaves.

Market entry, salary negotiations, legislative bargaining, chess openings, escalation games. Anywhere commitment and timing carry weight.

06

Simultaneous-Move Games

decide before you can see

Use for: price setting, announcements

Two rival phone makers set launch prices without seeing each other's choice. Each guesses what the other will do and commits at the same time. No reacting in the moment — decisions under pure uncertainty about the other side.

Written in normal (matrix) form:

$$G = \big(N, \{S_i\}_{i\in N}, \{u_i\}_{i\in N}\big)$$

Solved with the same Nash condition — best responses to beliefs about others.

Simultaneous bids, product launches, campaign announcements, standardized test-day strategies. Whenever nobody sees anybody's move first.

07

Mixed Strategies

randomness as a weapon

Use for: staying unpredictable

Penalty shootout. If the kicker always shoots left, the goalie learns the pattern. So the kicker mixes left, right and center — with calibrated probabilities. Randomness stops being a weakness and becomes the strategy itself.

A mixed strategy is a probability distribution over pure strategies:

$$\sigma_i \in \Delta(S_i), \qquad \mathbb{E}[u_i(\sigma)] = \sum_{s\in S} \sigma(s)\, u_i(s)$$

In equilibrium you mix so the opponent is indifferent between their options.

Penalty kicks, poker bluffing, security patrols, tax audits, rock-paper-scissors. Real pros randomize at almost exactly the theoretical rates.

08

Cooperative Game Theory

join forces, then split fairly

Use for: coalitions & joint ventures

Three construction firms bid on a mega-project none can finish alone. As a coalition they combine cranes, crews and know-how — then face the harder question: how to split the gains so nobody walks. Winning is easy; sharing is the game.

A coalition game assigns value to every group:

$$(N, v), \quad v: 2^N \to \mathbb{R}$$

The core keeps every coalition on board:

$$\sum_{i\in N} x_i = v(N), \qquad \sum_{i\in S} x_i \ge v(S)\ \ \forall S \subseteq N$$

Joint ventures, cost-sharing (airports, pipelines), political coalitions, profit splits. Also the home of the Shapley value — the "fair share by contribution" formula.

09

Bargaining Models

patience and outside options set the price

Use for: negotiation & deal-making

A buyer and seller haggle over a house. Seller starts high, buyer counters low, both drift toward the middle — but the final number depends less on "value" than on who can wait longer and who has better alternatives. Patience is leverage.

The Nash bargaining solution maximizes the product of gains over each side's fallback \(d\):

$$\max_{(u_1,u_2)\in F} (u_1 - d_1)(u_2 - d_2)$$

Better outside option \(d_i\) ⇒ bigger share. Improve your BATNA, win the deal.

Salary talks, M&A, trade deals, divorce settlements, hostage negotiations. The formula behind "never negotiate without an alternative."

10

Stackelberg Competition

the leader shapes the battlefield

Use for: leader–follower markets

A supermarket giant announces a milk price cut. Smaller stores watch, then set their own prices in response. Because the giant moved first — publicly and credibly — the followers react to its choice instead of choosing freely. The leader wrote the rules of the round.

The follower best-responds; the leader optimizes anticipating that response:

$$q_F^*(q_L) = \arg\max_{q_F} \pi_F(q_L, q_F)$$
$$q_L^* = \arg\max_{q_L} \pi_L\big(q_L,\, q_F^*(q_L)\big)$$

Platform pricing, supply chains, OPEC-style output leadership, first-mover product launches. First-mover advantage, formalized.
